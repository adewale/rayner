# Why does one version of a tune sound off the beat?

Listen to two versions of the same eight notes: C D E G E D C C. They use the same instrument and tempo. In the piano rolls, left to right is time, height is pitch, and each note's width is how long it sounds. The notes rise and fall in the same order, but their lengths differ. Does one version seem to pull against the beat? Where?

Count along: 1 2 3 4, 1 2 3 4. A click marks each beat, with a different click on the 1 that begins each bar. In A, a new note starts on every click. At the beginning of bar 2, A starts E. Which note starts there in B?

None. B holds its earlier E for one and a half beats instead of one. G therefore starts halfway through beat 4, before the bar line, and continues sounding across the next 1. The following E lasts only half a beat, bringing D back onto beat 2 of the second bar. Two changes in duration shift two note starts while leaving the rest of the tune on its original beats.

This is syncopation: note starts move from strong beats or parts of beats to weaker ones. Here G starts between clicks and holds across the strong 1 where A starts a new note. At 96 beats a minute a beat lasts 625 milliseconds, so G begins 312.5 milliseconds before the bar line; at 120 the gap shrinks to 250. The time changes with the tempo, but G's place, halfway through beat 4, does not.

Is the effect caused by the climb to G? Play both versions again with every pitch changed to C, keeping the starts and lengths. B still holds a note across the bar line instead of starting one on the 1. The red dots between beat lines mark the starts that moved. Removing the melody leaves the timing difference intact.

Now give B the same timing as A. Its starts line up with the clicks again, and the missing start at the bar line is gone. The comparison isolates the rhythm rather than the choice of pitches.

This tune uses two bars of four beats, with no rests and one loudness throughout. It shows one way to place notes against a beat, not every kind of syncopation. The score makes the shifted starts and held note visible; how strongly you hear their pull can vary.
