# Why does a flight from London to Los Angeles pass over Greenland?

Open the seat-back map on a flight from London to Los Angeles. The line it draws leaves Heathrow heading north-west, climbs past the southern tip of Greenland, crosses Hudson Bay and only then turns south toward California. Los Angeles is far to the south of London, yet the plane goes north. Lay a ruler across the same map from Heathrow to LAX and you get a tidy line heading west-south-west across the Atlantic. Which route is shorter?

The bent one, by a long way: 8,760 kilometres against 9,730. The ruler's line is about 970 kilometres longer. The flat map has bent the shortest route out of shape. On the globe the shortest route lies along the circle that a flat plane cuts when it passes through both cities and the centre of the Earth. Look at that circle from inside its plane and it is a straight line.

This kind of map, Mercator's, stretches every place sideways so that compass directions stay true, and the farther from the equator, the more it stretches: about 1.6 times at London's latitude, about twice where the route crosses Greenland. The Earth is narrower up there, so a route that goes north takes a shortcut, and the map draws that narrow part widest, so the shortcut looks like a detour. The ruler's line keeps one compass heading: easy to steer, longer to fly.

Now hold a globe, pin London and Los Angeles, and pull a string tight between them. A tight string takes the shortest path along the surface, and it swings north of both pins. Every point on the string lies on one flat sheet through London, Los Angeles and the centre of the globe. That sheet cuts the largest circle the sphere can hold, a great circle, and the shortest route between two places always runs along one.

So why does the string look curved? Turn the globe slowly. The route's arc flattens, then bends the other way. At one angle your eye lies in the sheet itself, and the route becomes a straight line through the middle of the globe. The route did not change as the globe turned; only where you stood did. The flat map is one more viewpoint, and one you cannot turn.

That also explains the northward swing. Both cities lie north of the equator, so the sheet through them and the Earth's centre tips toward the North Pole, and the great circle rises to its highest point between them: 62 degrees north, over southern Greenland. Lines of latitude are not great circles. Every line of latitude except the equator is a smaller circle, centred on the Earth's axis rather than its middle, so following one wastes distance.

How much it wastes depends on the route, and you can choose the second one. Madrid and New York lie almost on the same line of latitude. Heading due west from Madrid covers 5,930 kilometres; the great circle starts out west-north-west, reaches 46 degrees north and arrives after 5,760. The saving is smaller because the cities are closer together and farther from the pole. Put both cities on the equator instead and the saving vanishes: the equator is a great circle, so the ruler's line is the string, 6,670 kilometres either way. Only on the equator, or on a route running due north or south, do the two maps draw the same line.

Is there a flat map on which every shortest route is a ruler's line? Put the eye at the Earth's centre and project each place onto a flat sheet touching the globe. Every great circle lies in a plane through the eye, and that plane meets the sheet in a straight line. On this map, the gnomonic map, every shortest route is drawn straight: the turned globe's edge-on view, for every route at once. The price is that it cannot show even half the world.

The plane flies the great circle because on a round Earth it is the shortest way, and the map draws it bent because a flat map has to stretch a round world. Real flights leave the great circle to ride or avoid the jet stream, to stay within reach of airports and out of closed airspace, and the Earth's slightly flattened shape changes these distances by a fraction of a per cent. The shortcut over Greenland survives all of them.
