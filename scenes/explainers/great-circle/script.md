# Why does a flight from London to Los Angeles pass over Greenland?

Open the seat-back map on a flight from London to Los Angeles. The line it draws leaves Heathrow heading north-west, climbs past the southern tip of Greenland, crosses Hudson Bay and only then turns south toward California. Los Angeles is far to the south of London, yet the plane goes north. Lay a ruler across the same map from Heathrow to LAX and you get a tidy line heading west-south-west across the Atlantic. Which route is shorter?

The bent one, by a long way: 8,760 kilometres against 9,730. The ruler's line is about 970 kilometres longer. The flat map has bent the shortest route out of shape. On the globe the shortest route lies along the circle that a flat plane cuts when it passes through both cities and the centre of the Earth. Look at that circle from inside its plane and it is a straight line.

Start with why the map misleads. This kind of map, Mercator's, stretches every place sideways so that compass directions stay true. The farther from the equator, the more it stretches: around 1.6 times at London's latitude, about twice where the route crosses Greenland. The Earth really is narrower up there, so a route that goes north takes a shortcut across a narrower part of the world. The map draws that narrower part widest, so the shortcut looks like a detour. The ruler's line keeps one compass heading all the way, which makes it easy to steer and longer to fly.

Now hold a globe. Put a pin in London and another in Los Angeles, and pull a string tight between them. A tight string takes the shortest path along the surface, and you can watch it swing north of both pins. Every point on the string lies on one flat sheet through London, Los Angeles and the centre of the globe. Where that sheet meets the globe it cuts the largest circle the sphere can hold, with its centre at the centre of the Earth. That circle is called a great circle, and the shortest route between two places always runs along one.

So why does the string look curved? Turn the globe slowly. As it turns, the route's arc flattens, then bends the other way. At one angle your eye lies in the sheet itself, and the route becomes a straight line through the middle of the globe. The route did not change as the globe turned; only where you stood did. How bent a route looks depends on where you look from, not on the route. The flat map is one more viewpoint, and it is one you cannot turn.

That also explains the northward swing. Both cities lie north of the equator, so the sheet through them and the Earth's centre tips toward the North Pole, and the great circle rises to its highest point between them: 62 degrees north, over southern Greenland. Lines of latitude are not great circles. Every line of latitude except the equator is a smaller circle, centred on the Earth's axis rather than its middle, so following one wastes distance. Madrid and New York lie almost on the same line of latitude. Heading due west from Madrid covers 5,930 kilometres; the great circle starts out west-north-west, reaches 46 degrees north and arrives after 5,760. The saving is smaller because the two cities are closer together and farther from the pole.

Is there a flat map on which every shortest route is a ruler's line? Put the eye at the centre of the Earth and project each place outward onto a flat sheet touching the globe. Every great circle lies in a plane through the eye, and a plane through the eye meets the sheet in a straight line. On this map, the gnomonic map, every shortest route is drawn straight: it is the edge-on view from the turned globe, held still for every route at once. The price is that it cannot show even half the world, and it stretches shapes badly far from where the sheet touches. Navigators used both maps: they ruled the course on a gnomonic chart, copied its points onto a Mercator chart, and steered a few straight headings between them.

What if both cities sat on the equator? The equator is a great circle, so the shortest route would follow it, and Mercator's ruler line and the string would agree. Only on the equator, or on a route running due north or south, do the two maps draw the same line.

The plane flies the great circle because, on a round Earth, that is the shortest way, and the map draws it bent because a flat map has to stretch a round world. Real flights move off the great circle to ride or avoid the jet stream, to keep within reach of airports and to stay out of closed airspace, and the Earth's slightly flattened shape changes these distances by a fraction of a per cent. The shortcut over Greenland survives all of them.
