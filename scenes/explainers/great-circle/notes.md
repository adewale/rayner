# Construction and scope

The script is written to need 3D: the aha is a camera move, so the explainer cannot be a sequence of flat maps without losing its point.

The model is a sphere of mean radius 6,371 km. Airports are Heathrow (51.4700° N, 0.4543° W), LAX (33.9416° N, 118.4085° W), Madrid-Barajas (40.4983° N, 3.5676° W) and JFK (40.6413° N, 73.7781° W). Great-circle distance is by the haversine formula; the rhumb line by the Mercator-latitude formula. On those inputs: Heathrow–LAX 8,760 km against 9,733 km (11.1% longer), initial bearing 311.7°, highest latitude 62.27° N at 49.1° W; Madrid–JFK 5,760 km against 5,930 km, highest latitude 46.30° N at 38.9° W. "Due west" from Madrid is the rhumb line to JFK, which heads 270.15°, a sixth of a degree north of west, because JFK lies 0.14° north of Madrid. Mercator's east–west stretch is sec φ: 1.61 at Heathrow, 2.15 at the route's highest point, which the script rounds to "about twice" and the map labels ×2.1. The ellipsoid changes great-circle distances by 0.24% (Heathrow–LAX, 8,781 km on WGS84) and 0.25% (Madrid–JFK, 5,775 km): under the 0.6% the script's "a fraction of a per cent" allows. GeographicLib 2.0's geodesic solver is the independent oracle for those figures and for the sphere figures (`tools/oracle/great-circle.py` writes `test/fixtures/great-circle-geodesic.json`). "Over the southern tip of Greenland" and "across Hudson Bay" come from sampling the route against Natural Earth's 1:110m land polygons (`land.ts`, written by the same tool): it crosses Great Britain, Ireland, Greenland between 42.5° W and 49.3° W at about 62° N (its highest point is over Greenland), then the sea inside Hudson Bay between two stretches of North America. At this scale the coastline is coarse; the test asks only which landmass, not where its coast is.

The pictures are views of state: each step records `{ viewpoint, route }` (or `routes`, or the equator's `pairs`), and its picture is `seenFrom` of one model (graticule, coastlines, keyed city dots, the route, the ruler's line, the whole great circle and the sheet through it) through that viewpoint. Mercator's map, the globe and the gnomonic map are three viewpoint kinds of the same model; the gnomonic map is the eye at the Earth's centre. The turn step starts where the globe step left the eye and ends with the eye on the route's midpoint, which lies in the route's plane, so the route is seen edge-on; one `Follow` over the viewpoint's pitch goes 20° past it, where the arc bows the other way, and a second comes back. Seek equals play.

Two invariants hold on every frame of `rayner check`: nothing behind the globe is drawn in front (`depthOrder`), and a great circle seen edge-on, or on the gnomonic map, is drawn straight. Writing the first caught a real defect in `seenFrom` itself: the sheet's inner rim was clipped on chords that dip inside the sphere, drawing a sliver of the sheet's far side in front; cuts on segments between two points on the sphere are now made on the sphere. City dots keep their keys from map to globe, but a change of projection clears and enters rather than morphing: a morph draws its middle frames from no viewpoint, so parts on their way behind the globe would cross its face, which the depth check rejects. The globe's graticule starts at the equator because a southern parallel slipping over the horizon mid-turn would move its stroke's extent in one frame, which the motion check reports as a jump.

No lighting, shading or depth of field: they would add realism and nothing to the argument. Real flight tracks (winds, ETOPS, closed airspace) and the ellipsoid are named in the last step and not drawn.

Sources: GeographicLib (Karney 2013, "Algorithms for geodesics"; geographiclib 2.0 on PyPI), Snyder, "Map Projections: A Working Manual" (USGS Professional Paper 1395) for the Mercator and gnomonic formulas, and Natural Earth 1:110m land v5.1.2 (public domain).
