# Why does the host's rule change the odds of switching?

You pick door A. A car is behind one of three doors, with a goat behind each of the other two. The host opens B, reveals a goat and offers you C. Two doors remain closed. Would you switch? Would your answer change if the host had opened a door at random and happened to find a goat?

Start with a host who knows where the car is. He opens a goat door, tossing a coin if both remaining doors hide goats. The car was equally likely to be behind A, B or C before he opened anything.

Lay out every way the round can go as a strip, each cell as wide as its chance. If the car is behind A, the host can open B or C, so each of those rounds is a sixth. If the car is behind C, B is the one goat he can open, so that round fills a whole third. If the car is behind B, he has to open C. Before reading on, find the cells that could have shown you a goat behind B, and add up their widths.

Two cells match. In one, a sixth wide, the car is behind A and his coin happened to land on B. In the other, two sixths wide, the car is behind C and he had no other door to open. Of the three sixths left, switching wins in two. Its chance is 2 in 3, even though only two doors are still closed. The two doors are unequal because a car behind C forces him to open B, while a car behind A does so half the time.

Now change the host's rule. He chooses randomly between B and C without knowing where the car is. He might reveal the car, but this time he showed a goat at B. If the car is behind A, opening B still happens in one sixth of all rounds. If it is behind C, he opens B only half the time, giving another sixth; the other half reveals the car at C and ends the game. A car behind B cannot give the observation we saw.

Among this host's rounds that open B to a goat, staying and switching now each win one sixth, so they are equally likely. Compared with the informed host, switching has lost half its qualifying rounds, while staying has kept its sixth.

The two hosts can leave you looking at the same two closed doors with different odds. The goat behind B is the same evidence in both cases; what differs is how often each host's rule would have produced it, including the coin the informed host tosses when either goat is available. With these rules, switching wins 2 in 3 against the informed host and 1 in 2 against the random host, conditional on B opening to a goat. The comparison uses three doors, equally likely car locations and a first pick fixed at A.
