# Why does the host's rule change the odds of switching?

You pick door A. A car is behind one of three doors, with a goat behind each of the other two. The host opens B, reveals a goat and offers you C. Two doors remain closed. Would you switch? Would your answer change if the host had opened a door at random and happened to find a goat?

Start with a host who knows where the car is. He opens a goat door, choosing at random if both remaining doors hide goats. The car was equally likely to be behind A, B or C before he opened anything.

If the car is behind A, the host can open B or C, so he opens B in half of those rounds: one sixth of all rounds. If the car is behind C, he must open B: one third of all rounds. A car behind B cannot produce the goat you saw there.

Keep just the rounds that match what happened: B opened to a goat. One sixth of all rounds leaves the car at A; two sixths leave it at C. Of the three sixths remaining, switching wins in two. Its chance is 2 in 3, even though only two doors are still closed. The strip gives each kind of round a width proportional to its chance, so counting widths keeps these unequal chances visible.

Now change the host's rule. He chooses randomly between B and C without knowing where the car is. He might reveal the car, but this time he showed a goat at B. If the car is behind A, opening B still happens in one sixth of all rounds. If it is behind C, he opens B only half the time, giving another sixth; the other half reveals the car at C. A car behind B again cannot give the observation we saw.

Among this host's rounds that open B to a goat, staying and switching now each win one sixth. They are equally likely. Compared with the informed host, switching has lost half its qualifying rounds, while staying has kept its sixth.

The two hosts can leave you looking at the same two closed doors with different odds. What matters is the rule that produced the opened door, including how the informed host chooses when either goat is available. With these rules, switching wins 2 in 3 against the informed host and 1 in 2 against the random host, conditional on B opening to a goat. The comparison uses three doors, equally likely car locations and a first pick fixed at A.
