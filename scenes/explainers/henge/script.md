# Why does the sunset line up with Manhattan's streets?

Stand on 42nd Street and look toward the Hudson. On a Manhattanhenge evening, the low Sun fits between the buildings. The street will point the same way tomorrow. So why won't the Sun be in the same place?

"The Sun sets in the west" leaves out something important: which part of the western horizon? Manhattan's cross-streets point about 29 degrees north of due west. In winter, New York's Sun sets south of west. By the June solstice it sets 31.7 degrees north of west, two and a half degrees beyond the street's direction. The street is a fixed sightline; the sunset direction moves.

Look down on the grid from above, with north at the top. The cross-streets run from 29.1 degrees north of west at one end to 29.1 degrees south of east at the other. On an alignment evening the setting Sun lies on that line, so it shines straight down 42nd Street. That morning it rose 29.1 degrees north of east, 58.2 degrees away from the street's eastern end. The sunrise and the sunset mirror each other across north, while the street's two ends point in opposite directions, so only a street running due west and east can catch both on the same day. Manhattan's eastern ends face the sunrise only on a few winter mornings either side of the December solstice, when the Sun rises south of east.

That yearly movement comes from Earth's tilt. Its axis leans and keeps almost the same direction as Earth travels around the Sun. In June the Northern Hemisphere leans sunward. More of the Sun's daily path lies above New York's horizon, and it meets that horizon farther north. In December the north leans away: less of the path is above the horizon, and it meets it farther south. Earth's daily turning takes the Sun across the sky; tilt and the journey around the Sun change that path through the year.

Now mark where the low Sun appears each evening. From winter toward June, the marks travel north along the horizon. They pass the direction of 42nd Street, keep going, then turn back after the June solstice. On the southward journey they pass the street again. One fixed direction, crossed on the way out and on the way back: that gives Manhattan its two alignment seasons, in late May and July.

But the calendar names four evenings: half Sun and full Sun in each season. To see the difference, look down the street again. Left and right tell us whether the Sun is centred between the buildings. Up and down tell us how much of its disc is above the visible horizon. We need both.

Compare neighbouring evenings at the moment the Sun is centred along the street. In May, it is a little higher each evening: first its centre meets the horizon, leaving half the disc showing; then its bottom edge meets the horizon, leaving the full disc above it. In this model the two heights come about 1.2 days apart, roughly one evening to the next. In July that progression reverses. These are different heights on neighbouring days, rather than four separate sweeps across the horizon.

What if the street pointed due west? The two alignment seasons would move toward the spring and autumn equinoxes. Earth's motion would be unchanged; we would have changed the direction we are waiting for. Half and full Sun would also come closer together, less than half a day apart in this model, because near an equinox the Sun's path shifts fastest from one day to the next.

Manhattan supplies the sightline. Earth's tilt and orbit move the Sun's path across it and back. The disc and local horizon distinguish the named half- and full-Sun events. Exact viewing times still depend on the year and where you stand.
