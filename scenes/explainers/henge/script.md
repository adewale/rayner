# Why does the sunset line up with Manhattan's streets?

Stand on 42nd Street and look toward the Hudson. On a Manhattanhenge evening, the low Sun fits between the buildings. The street will point the same way tomorrow. So why won't the Sun be in the same place?

The street stays fixed while Earth's tilt moves the sunset direction across it and back. Look down on the grid from above, with north at the top. The cross-streets run from 29.1 degrees north of west at one end to 29.1 degrees south of east at the other. On an alignment evening the setting Sun lies on that line, so it shines straight down 42nd Street. That morning it rose 29.1 degrees north of east, 58.2 degrees away from the street's eastern end, which faces the sunrise only on a few winter mornings either side of the December solstice, when the Sun rises south of east.

"The Sun sets in the west" leaves out which part of the western horizon. Picture New York's sky as a dome: each day the Sun goes round a circle about the celestial pole, and the street rises from the horizon as a fixed arc. In June more of the day's circle lies above the horizon, and it meets the horizon 31.7 degrees north of west, two and a half degrees beyond the street. In December the shorter path meets it 31.7 degrees south of west. The circle shifts because Earth's axis is tilted: in June the north leans toward the Sun, in December away from it.

Now let the year run and mark where the low Sun appears each evening. From winter toward June, the marks travel north along the horizon. They pass the direction of 42nd Street, keep going, then turn back after the June solstice. On the southward journey they pass the street again. One fixed direction, crossed on the way out and on the way back: that gives Manhattan its two alignment seasons, in late May and July.

What if the street pointed due west? The two alignment seasons would move toward the spring and autumn equinoxes. Earth's motion would be unchanged; we would have changed the direction we are waiting for. Manhattan supplies the sightline, and Earth's tilt and orbit move the Sun's path across it and back. Exact viewing times still depend on the year and where you stand.

For readers who want more, three details follow the main story. The first shows the tilt from beside Earth's orbit: one axis direction, with the north leaning sunward in June and away in December, so more of New York's daily circle is in sunlight in June. The other two explain why the calendar names four evenings: half Sun and full Sun in each season. Stand at the dome's centre, in the middle of the street, and lower your eyes from the sky to the street. Left and right tell us whether the Sun is centred between the buildings; up and down tell us how much of its disc is above the visible horizon. In May the aligned Sun is a little higher each evening: first its centre meets the horizon, then its bottom edge, about 1.2 days apart in this model. In July that order reverses. For a due-west street the two come less than half a day apart, because near an equinox the Sun's path shifts fastest from one day to the next.
