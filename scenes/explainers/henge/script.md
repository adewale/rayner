# Why does sunset line up with Manhattan’s streets?

- **Reader:** someone who has heard of Manhattanhenge, or stood on a cross-street at sunset, and believes the Sun sets "in the west"; no astronomy assumed.
- **Answer:** The street stays fixed while Earth’s tilt moves the sunset direction across it and back
- **Changed condition:** the place. Moving from the Tudor City overpass on 42nd Street, which points 29.0° north of west, to Randolph Street at Michigan Avenue in Chicago, which points 0.2° south of west from 1.1° farther north, moves the 2026 alignments from 25–27 May and 15–16 July to 18–19 March and 23 September. The sky barely changes between the two cities: on 25 May the half Sun sets 29.7° to the right of Randolph Street, almost where it sets down 42nd Street. The street decides the dates.
- **Reader's own work:** predict whether the low Sun lines up again tomorrow, and how many times the sunset will cross the street in a year; switch the place to Chicago and see what moves and what does not; the two check questions transfer the crossing to other bearings.
- **Checked against:** Skyfield 1.55 observing the Sun through JPL’s DE440s ephemeris, recorded by `oracle/sun.py` into `fixtures/henge-sun.json`: the model’s solar position at 74 instants, day and night, to 0.015°; its half- and full-Sun moments on every evening of 2026 at both places to 5 seconds and 0.01°; and its alignment evenings, exactly. The places are OpenStreetMap nodes and each street’s bearing GeographicLib 2.1’s WGS84 geodesic between two of them, recorded in the same fixture. The Hayden Planetarium’s published 2026 minutes are a cross-check: at each the ephemeris puts the Sun 0.12°–0.20° north of 42nd Street, and its dates fall two to four days nearer the solstice than a flat horizon gives. The globe's coastlines are Natural Earth 1:110m (v5.1.2), written to `land.ts` by `oracle/land.py`. The idealised sky is checked against 48 vectors from ERFA’s `hd2ae` (pyerfa 2.0.1.5, `oracle/henge.py`, `fixtures/henge-erfa.json`) and USNO’s altitude formula. All in `model.test.ts`; `scene.test.ts` checks the pictures against the model.
- **Grounding:** recorded: Skyfield 1.55 with JPL’s DE440s, and ERFA’s horizon coordinates (pyerfa 2.0.1.5)
- **Leaves out:** the day’s weather, since refraction is the standard 34′ at the horizon; anything above the flat horizon, such as buildings beyond the street, the far shore or terrain, and the observer’s height above the street. A horizon raised half a degree moves the model’s May dates onto the Hayden Planetarium’s, which does not publish the horizon it uses. UT1 − UTC and the drift of ΔT, held at 69.2 s. The street views draw both streets as 42nd Street’s 30.5 m between two walls 60 m high; the overhead grid is schematic. The dome, the year’s turn, the camera’s descent and the tilted Earths use an idealised orbit (a fixed 23.44° tilt, circular), not the ephemeris, and the camera’s disc and buildings are schematic.

## Check yourself

1. Another city’s streets point 10° north of west, between Manhattan’s 29° and due west. Are its two alignment seasons nearer the June solstice than Manhattan’s, or farther from it?
   Farther from it. The sunset direction sweeps north from December to June and back; it passes 10° north of west before it reaches 29° on the way north, and after it on the way back, so the two seasons sit nearer the equinoxes.
2. Suppose another street points farther north than the northernmost sunset at its latitude. How many low-Sun alignments does it get?
   None. The annual curve must cross the street’s direction. Moving the street beyond its northern turning point removes both crossings.

## Why this explainer

The puzzle is that the street points the same way every day, yet the Sun fills it on only a few evenings. "The Sun sets in the west" hides which part of the western horizon, and the explainer replaces it with one picture: a fixed direction, and a sunset direction that sweeps north from December to June and back, crossing the street on the way out and on the way back. Two crossings, two seasons.

It starts at a real place on a real evening: the Tudor City overpass, the elevated spot on East 42nd Street that crowds use, at the moment of half Sun on 25 May 2026, seen through the street’s slot at true size. The next view adds a week either side and the next evening from the same spot, so the reader sees the Sun leave the slot within a day and set behind the buildings within a week. The globe then says where that direction comes from: from anywhere, the Sun sets toward the point on Earth where it stands overhead, and the street, carried on along the ground, meets the ring of such points at one latitude (21.2° N for 42nd Street, 0.5° S for Randolph Street), which the overhead point reaches twice a year. The dome and the year’s turn show the same sweep in the sky, and the chart gives the real 2026 evenings. Chicago is the counterfactual that makes the point stick: a street grid laid east–west at nearly the same latitude waits for the Sun at the equinoxes instead.

Every real position comes from a low-precision solar ephemeris in the model, checked against Skyfield and DE440s; the idealised fixed-tilt orbit is kept only for the dome, the year’s turn, the camera’s descent and the tilted Earths, which show geometry, not dates. Three steps are details (`kind: 'detail'`): the tilted Earths, which repeat the point the seasons explainer makes with its own globe, and the camera’s descent and the half- and full-Sun close views, which answer a second question (why the calendar names four evenings) once the main one is settled.

The street views and the camera are the sky seen from its centre (gnomonic), where great circles such as the horizon are straight lines; the dome is seen from outside (orthographic) and the globe from space. From outside, north is on the left, as the sky’s handedness requires; from the centre the model mirrors north and south so a camera facing west has north on its right. The year’s turn and the camera’s descent are held, frame by frame, to `depthOrder`, and from the centre the horizon and the street’s circle are held straight.
