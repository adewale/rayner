# Script: what puts an element in its group?

Questions the reader brings: Why is sodium in group 1 and chlorine in group 17? What do the columns of the periodic table mean? Why is helium over neon?

Take sodium's column: hydrogen 1, lithium 3, sodium 11, potassium 19, rubidium 37, caesium 55, francium 87. The jumps between neighbours are 2, 8, 8, 18, 18 and 32. Nothing in those numbers says "group 1", and the jumps are not a fixed step. Something other than the atomic number decides the column, and the reader is asked what makes those particular jumps.

Read the table's cells in order and each one adds an electron to a subshell: two in 1s, two in 2s, six in 2p, one in 3s for sodium. Runs of one colour are subshells. Colour the whole table by the subshell its last electron entered and it falls into blocks: 2 columns of s, 10 of d, 6 of p, with the f block's 14 set below. That is the answer: an element's group is the column where its last electron lands, in rows of 2 s, 10 d and 6 p columns. Chlorine's last electron is the fifth in 3p, so it lands in column 2 + 10 + 5 = 17 even though rows 2 and 3 have no d electrons. Helium is the one exception: its 1s² would land in column 2, but it completes the first shell, so the table puts it with the other full shells.

The same answer works as a count. Past the preceding noble gas, sodium has one electron and is in group 1; iron has 4s² 3d⁶, 8, and is in group 8. Chlorine counts only 7, ten short, because its row lacks the 10 d columns. Copper is listed by NIST as 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, not the filling rule's 4s² 3d⁹, but both hold 11 electrons, so it stays in group 11.

Back to the opening jumps. Every member of a column ends the same way, one row further on, so each jump is a whole row: 8 = 2 + 6, 18 = 2 + 10 + 6, 32 = 2 + 14 + 10 + 6. The jumps are the block widths added up.

**The aha:** the 18 columns are the subshells laid side by side (2 + 10 + 6), and the uneven jumps in the opening are those same widths summed into rows.

**Sentence to remember:** an element's group is the column where its last electron lands, in rows of 2 s, 10 d and 6 p columns.

**Check questions**, in `meta.checks`, asked after the last step:

1. Group 1's atomic numbers jump by 2, 8, 8, 18, 18 and 32. Where does 18 come from?
2. Chlorine has 7 electrons past neon's core. Why is it in group 17, not group 7?
3. The filling rule predicts 4s² 3d⁹ for copper; NIST lists 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. Which group does each put it in?
4. Helium's 1s² ends like beryllium's 2s². Why does the table put helium over neon instead?

**Cases:** sodium (the plain case), chlorine (the count is 10 short), iron (the d block; ruthenium's different ending still counts 8), copper (NIST's exception changes the ending, not the group) and helium (the full shell overrides the count). Names and the model's limits follow the main story as details.
