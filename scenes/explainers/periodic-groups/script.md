# Why does the periodic table have this shape?

Sodium is element 11, potassium 19 and rubidium 37. They share a column, but the jumps are 8, then 18. If every row of the table held the same number of elements, every jump down a column would be the same. So the lower rows must be longer. Why?

For a neutral atom, the atomic number also counts electrons, so element 11 has 11. A useful filling rule adds those electrons in runs of 2, 6, 10 and 14. The lengths and the order of the runs are our starting information: we will see what they imply for the table, not where the rule comes from.

Give each element a cell and lay the runs beside one another, starting a new row at each run of 2. The rows come out 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 cells long, and every jump down sodium's column is one of those lengths. Sodium's eleventh electron is the first cell of a new run of 2. What shape appears if runs of the same length share columns?

Put the runs of 2 at the left, the runs of 6 at the right and the runs of 10 between them. The rows with no run of 10 now have a ten-column gap. The longest rows also contain a run of 14, and lifting those runs into two rows below the table keeps it 18 columns wide. The familiar gap at the top and the two detached rows have appeared from the run lengths and one rule for lining them up.

The runs have names: subshells, named by the shell number and a letter for the length, s, p, d and f for 2, 6, 10 and 14. By the filling rule, sodium's runs read 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. One element is still out of place. Its run of 2 leaves helium over beryllium, but the table puts it over neon: helium's two electrons complete its first shell, as neon's eight complete its second. That grouping is chemistry we bring to the table; sliding cells does not produce it.

The rest is detail for a reader who wants it. Which run fills next follows an empirical rule: lowest n + l first, lower n on a tie, which is why iron's 4s fills before its 3d. Down a column, the electrons beyond the previous noble gas usually end the same way; where they do not, as with iron's 3d⁶ 4s² and ruthenium's 4d⁷ 5s¹, they still add up to the same count. The filling rule is empirical and has exceptions: copper's listed configuration moves an electron from its run of 2 into its run of 10, and still has 11 electrons beyond argon, so its column does not change. The rule reconstructs this layout; it does not explain atomic structure or chemical behaviour.
