When should a short-lived certificate renew?

Your renewal setting was written when certificates lasted ninety days. Lifetimes are now being cut in steps, toward 47 days for every public TLS certificate and as little as six days for some. Here is what a setting like "renew 30 days before expiry" actually does as that happens, and what to use instead.

Short answer
  1. Set the renewal point as a share of the lifetime, not a number of days. Renew when about one third of the lifetime is left. For a 90-day certificate that is the familiar "30 days before expiry". For a 47-day certificate it is about 16 days, and for a 6-day certificate it is about 2 days. For certificates shorter than about ten days, renew at half the lifetime.
  2. If your client and CA support ARI (ACME Renewal Information, RFC 9773), let the CA's suggested window decide, and keep the one-third rule as the fallback.
  3. Run the check often: at least twice a day, and every hour or few hours for very short certificates. The time between the renewal point and expiry is all the time you have to notice and fix a failure, and it shrinks along with the lifetime.
  4. Alert on the certificate actually being served, not on whether the job ran. Something like "less than one sixth of the lifetime left" means renewal should have happened and didn't.

Three numbers describe every renewal policy

Each certificate has a lifetime, from notBefore to notAfter. Your renewal job picks a renewal point somewhere inside that span. Everything after the renewal point is your margin: the time you have to retry, notice an error, fix DNS or a firewall, and try again before the old certificate expires.

The setting you have, such as Certbot's renew_before_expiry = 30 days, fixes the margin as a number of days. That worked because lifetimes stayed at 90 days for years, so 30 days of margin always meant "the last third". When the lifetime changes and the margin doesn't, the renewal point moves to a different place.

What a fixed "30 days before expiry" does as lifetimes shrink

Two things happen, in order:

  1. Above 30 days, it works but wastes certificates. On a 47-day certificate, "30 days before expiry" means renewing on day 17, barely a third of the way through. Nothing breaks, but you issue about twice as many certificates as you need, and each extra issuance is another chance for a validation failure or rate limit.
  2. At 30 days or less, it fails abruptly. A certificate with a lifetime of 30 days or less already has 30 days or less left when it is issued. It is born inside the renewal window. Each time the job runs, it finds the certificate "due" and renews it again. With a twice-daily timer that is about 730 issuances a year for one name. A CA will rate-limit that (Let's Encrypt, for example, limits how many certificates can be issued for the same set of names per week). Then renewals start failing, and once they keep failing the certificate expires.

The failure doesn't build up gradually. The setting looks fine until the lifetime drops to or below the fixed number, and then it flips straight to renewing on every run.

The opposite mistake: renewing on a calendar

Some setups don't check expiry at all. They run a forced renewal "every 60 days" from cron or a CI schedule. On 90-day certificates that left 30 days spare. On 47-day certificates the certificate expires on day 47 and the next renewal doesn't come until day 60, so the site is broken for 13 days of every cycle. If that's your setup, replace it with a frequent check that renews only when the certificate is due.

Why one third, and why half for very short certificates

There are two costs you're trading against each other:

  • Renew too early and you issue more certificates than necessary. That adds load on the CA, uses up rate limits, and adds more chances to fail validation.
  • Renew too late and the margin is too short to recover from a bad day. CA outages, DNS changes, an expired API token for DNS-01 or a changed firewall rule can each take hours or days to notice and fix.

Renewing with one third left means each certificate is replaced after it has served two thirds of its life. The margin is still twice as long as you will usually need. That ratio is what you were already using on 90-day certificates. It's also the default in several clients: Caddy renews with a third of the lifetime left, cert-manager renews at two thirds of the duration unless told otherwise, and recent Certbot releases use a proportional rule when renew_before_expiry isn't set.

For a six-day certificate, one third left is only two days. That's a weekend, which isn't enough to recover from much. Renewing at half gives three days. Since these certificates are short by design, issuing them a bit more often costs little, and the CAs offering them recommend renewing around the halfway point.

This is the part a 90-day setting never had to deal with. A failure that took a week to notice was fine with 30 days of margin. With 16 days it's tight, and with 2 days the certificate has expired. As lifetimes shrink, the parts you need to change are how often the job runs and how quickly someone is alerted, not only the threshold.

ARI: letting the CA choose the moment

ACME Renewal Information (RFC 9773) lets your client ask the CA, for each certificate, "when should I renew this?" The CA replies with a time window. A good client picks a random moment inside it.

ARI fixes things a local threshold can't. It spreads renewals out so they don't all hit the CA at once. It also lets the CA ask for early replacement after a mis-issuance, and some CAs exempt ARI-guided renewals from their rate limits. For ARI to help, your client still needs to check often, because a window that moves earlier only matters if the job runs before the old date. Keep a proportional fallback for when the CA doesn't answer.

What to change in practice

Certbot

Look in /etc/letsencrypt/renewal/<name>.conf (and cli.ini) for a fixed setting:

# written for 90-day certificates: means "the last third" only at 90 days
renew_before_expiry = 30 days

On a current Certbot release, the simplest fix is to delete that line so the built-in proportional default (and ARI, where supported) applies. Run certbot --version and read the release notes for your version to confirm what its default is. If you must keep an explicit value, choose one smaller than the shortest lifetime you'll get, and revisit it each time the lifetime drops. Then confirm the timer runs at least twice a day (systemctl list-timers | grep certbot, or check your cron entry), and test with certbot renew --dry-run.

Other clients

  • cert-manager: prefer renewBeforePercentage over a fixed renewBefore, or leave both unset to get the default of renewing at two thirds of the duration.
  • acme.sh, lego, custom scripts: find the "days" threshold (often 30) or a cron job that forces renewal on a calendar. Replace it with a frequent job that compares remaining time to a share of the certificate's own lifetime, calculated from notBefore and notAfter, or that uses ARI if supported.
  • Hard-coded "every 60 days" schedules in CI or cron: remove them. They will break as soon as the lifetime drops below the interval.

Monitoring

Probe the certificate your endpoints actually serve. A renewed certificate on disk that the web server never reloaded doesn't help. Alert when the remaining time falls below about half the renewal margin (for example, one sixth of the lifetime). By then the job has had many tries and failed, so a person needs to act while there's still time.

The rule, in one line

renew when  remaining <= lifetime / 3      (lifetime / 2 if lifetime < ~10 days)
            or ARI window has opened
check       every few hours, never less than twice a day
alert when  remaining <= lifetime / 6      (on the certificate actually served)
